2.1 Tectonic setting
The Circum-Adriatic region is one of the most tectonically complex areas in Europe, shaped by the convergence of Africa, Adria, and Eurasia [picardi2011]. The region can be divided into several tectonic domains organised around a shared continental foreland, the Adriatic (Adria) Plate, which underlies the surrounding orogenic belts [menicheli2025, Orecchio2023]. Adria behaves as an independent microplate, moving separately from both Eurasia and Africa, with a lithospheric thickness of 100–120 km — thinner than the adjacent European lithosphere [Kissling2024, facenna2014]. Modern geodetic and space-geodetic data indicate that the northern part of Adria is undergoing counterclockwise rotation relative to Eurasia [Satoli2007].

Along the northern margin of Adria, the Alps form a high-elevation, double-vergent collisional belt trending broadly E–W. The orogen consists of a complex stack of European crystalline basement and its Permo-Mesozoic sedimentary cover juxtaposed against Adria-derived units. The Alps formed during Cretaceous–Eocene/Oligocene continent–continent collision, representing the northward indentation of Adria into the European margin following closure of the Alpine Tethys [carmiati2012, elsharkawy2020]. Present-day deformation is concentrated along the Southern Alpine Front, which accommodates N–S contraction at ~40 nanostrain/yr, while the Western Alps experience arc-normal extension [picardi2011].

Along the western margin, the Apennines form an accretionary wedge produced by subduction and delamination of Adriatic lithosphere [carmiati2012]. The wedge comprises Mesozoic–Cenozoic cover sequences scraped off the downgoing plate and decoupled from the underlying Variscan basement [facenna2014]. The belt formed rapidly after the late Eocene–Oligocene, following a subduction polarity flip in which east-directed Alpine subduction was replaced by west-directed Apennine subduction, driven by rollback and delamination of Adriatic lithosphere. The Apennines are characterised by a paired tectonic regime: SW–NE extension and normal faulting along the axial ridge, coeval with compression and thrusting beneath the Po Plain and Adriatic offshore [salimbeni2022]. At depth, a slab window occurs at 70–200 km beneath the central-southern Apennines, where the slab has detached, while the slab remains attached beneath the northern Apennines [elsharkawy2020].

On the opposite, northeastern margin of Adria, the **Dinarides** form a SW-vergent, NW–SE trending fold-and-thrust belt. The external portions are dominated by thick Mesozoic–Paleozoic shallow-marine carbonate platform rocks on a crystalline basement [Orecchio2023, facenna2014]. The belt initiated during Late Jurassic obduction of Vardar ophiolites and evolved through Cretaceous–Paleogene underthrusting and collision of Adria beneath Eurasia. Unlike the Apennines, the Dinarides did not undergo widespread slab rollback; instead, flat subduction with underplating of continental lithosphere occurred [pearce2012]. In the north, deformation transitions to a transpressional regime as the rotating Adria microplate collides obliquely with the Alps. Modern GPS velocities indicate slow contractional deformation (<20 nanostrains/yr) across the external Dinarides, underlain by a laterally continuous slab imaged to 300 km depth, with a shallow slab gap beneath the northernmost segment attributed to late Eocene–Oligocene slab break-off [salimbeni2022].

The Albanides form the direct structural continuation of the Dinarides, sharing the same SW-vergent, NW–SE trend, and separated from them by the Shkodër-Peja Normal Fault (SPNF), which overprints the older transverse zone to accommodate active, scissor-like orogen-parallel extension [handy2019]. The internal domain preserves slices of Middle Jurassic Neotethyan Mirdita ophiolite, while the external units comprise the Krasta, Kruja, Ionian, and Sazani (Pre-Apulian) zones — Meso-Cenozoic carbonate platforms and pelagic basinal sequences deposited on Adria's passive margin. The system developed through progressive accretion of these passive-margin units from the Late Cretaceous onward. At depth, deep crustal underplating has built crustal-scale ramps, driving a late Miocene–Pliocene exhumation pulse of ~3–4 km in the hinterland. Active deformation is expressed as NE–SW compressional and strike-slip seismicity along the coast.

At the southeastern margin of Adria, the **Hellenides** form a highly active, NW–SE trending subduction–collision system extending from the central Adriatic Sea to Crete [kiraly2018]. The external belt consists of thick Mesozoic carbonate platform and basinal units; the internal zones expose Paleozoic basement and ophiolitic fragments [facenna2010]. The Hellenides formed through ongoing subduction of African and Ionian lithosphere beneath Eurasia since the Eocene–Oligocene, accompanied by ~50° of clockwise oroclinal bending driven by slab retreat [tsanakas2025, menicheli2025]. The subduction zone is bounded to the northwest by the dextral Kefalonia Transform Fault, a proto-STEP fault separating continental (Apulian) subduction in the north from oceanic subduction in the south [bird2003, kiraly2018]. This segment is highly seismically active, with deformation in the overriding plate dominated by rapid crustal extension.

2.2. Volcanism 
Active magmatism in the Circum-Adriatic region is highly localised, restricted to domains of active subduction rollback or lithospheric rifting, concentrated in two zones: the Apennine (Italian) and Hellenic systems. The Italian Volcanic Province comprises three provinces: the Aeolian Arc (Stromboli, Vulcano), producing calc-alkaline magmas from active subduction and rollback of the Ionian oceanic slab beneath the Calabrian Arc; the Roman Volcanic Province (Vesuvius, Campi Flegrei), exhibiting ultrapotassic to potassium-rich magmatism from melting of a mantle wedge hydrated by slab-derived fluids; and anorogenic volcanism at Mt. Etna and the Sicily Channel centres (Pantelleria, Linosa), showing alkaline to tholeiitic compositions driven by lithospheric extension and asthenospheric upwelling through transtensional slab-edge tears [facenna2014, salimbeni2022].

The Hellenic Volcanic Arc is concentrated along the South Aegean Volcanic Arc (Santorini, Milos, Nisyros, Methana), producing calc-alkaline lavas and pyroclastics from northeastward subduction of the dense, old Ionian oceanic lithosphere. In northeastern Greece and the Aegean islands, Oligocene-to-Pleistocene volcanism hosted extensive epithermal mineralising systems, generating colloform-crustiform, amethyst-bearing veins from near-neutral to alkaline fluids.

By contrast, the Alps, northern Apennines, and Dinarides are primarily accretionary/collisional wedges that grow by stacking buoyant, non-volcanic continental basement and Mesozoic carbonate platforms. Because subduction beneath these sectors has slowed or ceased (slab break-off or continental collision), there is no flux of subduction fluids to trigger mantle melting, rendering these belts tectonically dry and non-volcanic [orecchio2023].

2.3 Geomorphology setting
The Circum-Adriatic region is shaped by active tectonism and surface processes, expressed across several morphological domains.

Sedimentary basins and plains include: the Po Basin, north of the Apennines, a foredeep filled with 8–12 km of Pliocene–Quaternary alluvial sediment; the Pannonian Basin, east of the Dinarides, an extensive back-arc basin filled with thick Neogene siliciclastic deposits; the Moesian Platform in northern Bulgaria, a stable, low-relief platform mantled by flat-lying Mesozoic–Tertiary carbonates and Quaternary loess; and the Adriatic Foreland, a stable, low-relief marine basin.

Rift and extensional basins include: the Corinth Rift, a highly active extensional graben with a rapid N–S stretching rate, preserving alternating marine and lacustrine syn-rift sediments controlled by steep normal faults; the Tyrrhenian Basin, a deep back-arc marine basin floored by thin oceanic crust and surrounded by thinned continental margins; and the Sicily Channel, which hosts active extensional grabens and volcanic centres.

Karst terrain: the Classical and Dinaric Karst, widespread across the External Dinarides, developed on Mesozoic carbonate platform sequences. High water flux and carbonate solubility have produced extensive cave networks, dolines, and large closed depressions (poljes).

Mountain relief: the region's topography reflects active collision, deep crustal underplating, and localised mantle dynamics. The Alps form a high-altitude, double-vergent belt (average elevation ≈1.5 km) where erosion has exposed crystalline basement across >40% of the chain, with the primary drainage divide aligned with the highest peaks. The Apennines are younger and morphologically lower (≈0.5 km average elevation). The Dinarides show rugged, deeply incised topography dominated by Mesozoic carbonate sequences, transitioning southward into the Albanides, where crustal underplating has built crustal-scale ramps. Relief culminates in Greece at Mount Olympus (2,917 m) and the Pindos range, which preserve localised glacial landforms (cirques, moraine deposits) from Late Pleistocene glaciation.

Coastlines preserve strong geomorphological indicators of Quaternary eustatic sea-level change and active vertical tectonism. The irregular Greek coastline exemplifies this variability: 52.6% is low-slope (<10°), dominated by depositional coastal plains, dunes, and river deltas, while 19.5% is steep (>30°) with prominent cliffs. Active uplift along these cliffed margins has preserved stepped sequences of Late Pleistocene marine terraces alongside Holocene uplifted and submerged tidal notches. The low-lying, low-energy sandy Italian Adriatic coast is dominated by deltaic sedimentation (e.g. the Po Delta) and active subsidence — in contrast to the jagged, longitudinal Balkan Dalmatian coast, where marine transgression over active NW–SE Dinaric thrust sheets has flooded intervening valleys, producing a parallel network of elongated islands and channels.
