2.1 Tectonic setting
The Circum-Adriatic region represents a highly dynamic belt located in the heart of south-central Europe. This region is one of the most tectonically complex region in Europe due to the convergence between Africa, Adria, and Eurasia [picardi2011]. The quarternary Circum-Adriatic region can be divided into several tectonic domains. The Adriatic Plate is a continental block that acts as the shared foreland for sorrounding orogenic belts[menicheli2025,Oreccio2023]. The plates moving independently of both Eurasia and Africa as an independent microplate. Modern geodetic and space geodetic data confirm that the northern part of the Adria plate is undergoing a counterclockwise rotation relative to Eurasia around an Eulerian pole in the Western Alps It has a thickness of 100-120 km, which is thinner than the adjacent European lithosphere [piromallo2003,facenna2014]. 

Along the northern margin of the Adria plate, the Alps form a highly elevated, double-vergent collisional orogenic belt with generally E-W orientation. The orogen is composed of a complex stacking of the European crystalline basement and its Permo-Mesozoic sedimentary cover, juxtaposed against Adria-derived units. The Alps formed during the Cretaceous to Eocene and Oligocene continent-continent collision, representing the northward indentation of the Adria plate into the European margin after the complete closure of the Alpine Tethys Ocean [carmiati2012, elsharkawy2020].  Presently, the Alps accommodate active N-S contractional deformation and shortening along the Southern Alpine Front at a rate of 1–3 mm/yr, while the Western Alps experience arc-normal extension [picardi2011].

In the western margin of the Adria plate, lies the Apennines, which an accretionary wedge formed by subduction and delamination of Adriatic lithosphere[carmiati2012]. This wedge is composed of slices of Mesozoic to Cenozoic sedimentary cover sequences that have been scraped off the downgoing plate and stacked, decoupled from the underlying Variscan basement [facenna214]. The chain formed rapidly after the late Eocene-Oligocene as a result of a fundamental subduction flip, where east-directed Alpine subduction was replaced by west-directed Apennines subduction driven by the rollback and delamination of the Adriatic lithosphere. The Apennines are characterized by a highly active, paired tectonic regime, exhibiting SW-NE oriented across-belt extension and normal faulting along the axial ridge, and coeval compression and shortening along the buried frontal thrusts in the Po Plain and Adriatic offshore [salimbeni2022]. The deep the distructure is marked by a prominent slab window at 70 - 200 km depth beneath central-southern Apennines where the slab has detached, while beneath the northern Apennines, the slab remains attached[elsharkawy2020].

On the opposite, In the northeastern margin of Adriatic plates, the Dinarides form a southwest-vergent, NW-SE trending fold-and-thrust belt. The external portions of the belt are dominated by exceptionally thick Mesozoic to Paleozoic shallow-marine carbonate platform rocks deposited on a crystalline basement[Oreccio2023, facenna2014].  The belt initiated during the Late Jurassic obduction of Vardar ophiolites and evolved through Cretaceous-Paleogene underthrusting and collision of the Adriatic plate beneath Eurasia. In contrast to the Apennines, the Dinarides did not undergo widespread slab rollback; instead, they experienced a flat subduction characterized by the underplating of continental lithospheric material [pearce2012]. In the northern sector, the deformation transitions into a transpressional regime as the rotating Adria microplate collides obliquely with the Alps. Modern GPS velocities describe slow contractional deformation rates (<20 nanostrains/yr) across the external Dinarides, which are underlain by a laterally continuous slab imaged down to 300 km depth, though a shallow slab gap exists beneath the northernmost segment where late Eocene-Oligocene slab break-off occurred [sambelini2022].

The Albanides represent a fold-and-thrust system in Albania, representing the direct structural continuation of the Dinarides. They are separated by the Skhoder-Peja Normal Fault (SPNF), which cuts directly across the fold-and-thrust belt and overprints the older transverse zone to accommodate active, scissor-like orogen-parallel extension [handy2019]. The internal domain preserves slices of the Middle Jurassic Neotethyan Mirdita ophiolite, while the external units comprise the Krasta, Kruja, Ionian, and Sazani (Pre-Apulian) zones, consisting of Meso-Cenozoic carbonate platforms and pelagic basinal sequences deposited on Adria's passive margin. The system developed through progressive accretion of Adria's passive margin units since the Late Cretaceous. At depth, the system is dominated by deep crustal underplating that has built crustal-scale ramps, driving a pronounced late Miocene-Pliocene exhumation pulse of approximately 3–4 km in the hinterland. The active deformation is characterized by NE-SW oriented compressional and strike-slip seismicity along the coast.

The Hellenides represent the highly active, NW-SE trending subduction-collision system at the southeastern margin of Adria, extending from the central Adriatic Sea to Crete [kiraly2018]. The external belt consists of thick Mesozoic carbonate platform and basinal units, while the internal zones expose Paleozoic basement and ophiolitic fragments [facenna2010]. The Hellenides formed by ongoing subduction of the African and Ionian lithosphere beneath Eurasia since the Eocene-Oligocene, a process accompanied by clockwise oroclinal bending of approximately 50° driven by slab retreat [tsanakas2025,menicheli2025]. The subduction zone is bounded to the northwest by the dextral Kefalonia Transform Fault, which acts as a proto-STEP fault separating continental (Apulia) subduction in the north from oceanic subduction in the south [bird2003,kiraly2018].


2.2. Volcanism 
Active magmatism and volcanism in the Circum-Adriatic region are highly localized, restricted to domains of active subduction rollback or lithospheric rifting. There are two zones with active volcanism, Apennines and Hellenic. The Italian Volcanic province consist of : The Aeolian Arc, volcanoes such as Stromboli and Vulcano produce calc-alkaline magmas representing active subduction and rollback of the Ionian oceanic slab beneath the Calabrian arc; The Roman Volcanic Province with systems like Vesuvius and Campi Flegrei exhibit ultrapotassic to potassium-rich magmatism, driven by melting of a mantle wedge heavily hydrated by fluids released from the subducting slab; The Anorogenic Volcanism with Mt. Etna and the volcanic centers of the Sicily Channel (Pantelleria, Linosa) exhibit alkaline to tholeiitic compositions, which are driven by lithospheric extension, rifting, and asthenospheric mantle upwelling through transtensional tears along slab edges [facenna2014, sambelini2022].

The Hellenic Volcanic Arc has active volcanism is concentrated along the South Aegean Volcanic Arc (Santorini, Milos, Nisyros, Methana), producing calc-alkaline lavas and pyroclastics driven by the northeastward subduction of the dense, old Ionian oceanic lithosphere. In northeastern Greece and Aegean islands, Oligocene-to-Pleistocene volcanism hosted extensive epithermal mineralizing systems, which generated colloform-crustiform amethyst-bearing veins from near-neutral to alkaline fluids .

Orogens like the Alps, Northern Apennines, and Dinarides are primarily accretionary and collisional wedges. They grow by scraping off and stacking highly buoyant, non-volcanic continental basement slices and Mesozoic carbonate platforms. Because subduction has slowed down or ceased beneath these sectors (due to slab break-off or continental collision), there is no flux of subduction fluids to trigger mantle melting, rendering these mountain chains tectonically dry and non-volcanic [orecchio2023]

2.3 Geomorphology setting
The Circum-Adriatic region is shaped by active tectonism and surface process, there are several morphological domains. There are several sedimentary basins and plains in this region, namely: The Po Basin at the north of Apennine swhich is a massive foredeep basin filed with 8 to 12 km of Pliocene-Quarternary alluvial sediment; The Pannonian Basin in the east of Dinarides which is an extensive back arc basin filled with thick Neogene siliclastic deposits; The Moesian Platform in northern Bulgaria, which a stable low releif platform mantled by flat-lying, shelf-type Mesozoic-Tertiary carbonates and Quaternary loess deposits; and Adriatic Foreland which a stable, low-relief marine basin.

This region has several rift and extensional basin, which are: The Corinth Rift, which a highly active extensional graben with rapid N-S stretching rate, preserving alternating marine and lacustrine syn-rift sediments controlled by steep normal faults; The Tyrrhenian Basin, which is a deep back-arc marine basin floored by thin oceanic crust and sorrounded by thinned continental margins; The Sicily Channel, which is an active extensional grabens hosting rift basins and volcanic centers.

The Classical and Dinaric Karst: Widespread across the External Dinarides, this landscape is developed on Mesozoic carbonate platform sequences. High water flux and carbonate solubility have created extensive cave networks, dolines, and large closed depressions called poljes.

The mountainous terrain of the Circum-Adriatic region reflects a dramatic geomorphic diversity shaped by the active collision of plates, deep-seated crustal underplating, and localized mantle dynamics. In the north, the Alps form a high-altitude, double-vergent collisional belt (average elevation ≈1.5 km) where intense erosion has exposed hard, crystalline basement rocks across more than 40% of the chain, and the primary drainage divide aligns directly with the highest peaks. In contrast, the Apennines are younger and morphologically lower (average elevation ≈0.5 km). The Dinarides showcase a rugged, deeply incised topography dominated by massive Mesozoic carbonate sequences. This system transitions southward into the Albanides, where deep-seated crustal underplating has built crustal-scale ramps.

The coastlines of the Circum-Adriatic region preserve exceptional geomorphological indicators of rapid Quaternary eustatic sea-level fluctuations and active vertical tectonism. The highly irregular Greek coastline exemplifies this morphological variability, with 52.6% classified as low-slope coasts (<10∘) dominated by depositional coastal plains, dunes, and river deltas, while steep coasts (>30∘) make up 19.5% and feature prominent coastal cliffs. Active tectonic uplift along these cliffed margins has preserved step-like sequences of Late Pleistocene uplifted marine terraces alongside Holocene uplifted and submerged tidal notches.  Furthermore, the low-lying, low-energy sandy Italian Adriatic coast is dominated by deltaic sedimentation (such as the Po Delta) and active subsidence, standing in stark contrast to the highly jagged, longitudinal Balkan Dalmatian coast, where marine transgression over active NW-SE trending Dinaric thrust sheets has flooded intervening valleys to create a parallel network of elongated islands and marine channels.
