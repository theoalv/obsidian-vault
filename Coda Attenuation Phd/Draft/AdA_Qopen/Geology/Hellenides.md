Present-day and Cenozoic magmatism and volcanism in the Hellenides are driven by the ongoing northeastward subduction and rapid southwestward slab rollback of the African and Ionian lithospheres beneath the overriding Aegean-Eurasian plate. Driven by this back-arc extension, volcanic centers have migrated diachronously southward since the late Eocene-Oligocene (~30–35 Ma), transitioning from Late Cretaceous to Oligocene magmatic belts in the northern Rhodope Massif down through Miocene Aegean Island volcanic fields (such as Lesvos and Limnos), and culminating in the modern, active South Aegean Volcanic Arc. This active arc is defined by volcanic and geothermal centers located at Sousaki, Methana, Milos, Santorini, and Nisyros, with Santorini and Nisyros exhibiting the highest rates of active seismotectonic deformation, geodetic unrest, and geothermal flux.
Geochemically, the magmatic suites are dominated by intermediate to acidic calc-alkaline, high-K calc-alkaline, and shoshonitic lavas and pyroclastics—ranging from metaluminous to slightly peraluminous andesites and dacites to rhyolites—which are generated by subduction fluid dehydration that lowers the solidus of the overriding mantle wedge. These volcanic units host highly developed low- to intermediate-sulfidation epithermal precious- and base-metal systems characterized by extensive hydrothermal alteration halos, including propylitic, sericitic, adularia, and zeolitic types. A highly notable feature of these Greek Cenozoic volcanic systems is the widespread occurrence of gem-quality, deep-violet amethyst (e.g., at Megala Therma in the flanks of the Stypsi stratovolcano on Lesvos; Kassiteres, Kirki, and Kornofolia in Western Thrace; and Chondro Vouno and Kalogries on Milos Island), which precipitated at shallow subaerial to shallow-submarine depths from relatively cool (~200–250 °C) and low-salinity hydrothermal fluids undergoing gradual cooling, boiling, and mixing with meteoric or seawater
